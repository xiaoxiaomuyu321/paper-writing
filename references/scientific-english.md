# Scientific English and bilingual revision

For the deeper, book-grounded sentence/paragraph/style techniques, see [craft/language-craft.md](craft/language-craft.md) — distilled from Gastel & Day and Turabian and the authority on language craft for every journal.

## Preserve meaning first

For editing or Chinese-to-English translation, create a terminology list for key entities, variables, methods, groups, and abbreviations. Preserve logical strength: “相关” is not automatically “causes,” “提高” needs a stated comparator, and “显著” means statistically significant only when supported by a defined test. Keep all numerical values, signs, confidence intervals, units, chemical/gene names, and citations unchanged unless correction is verified.

## Sentence and paragraph choices

- Prefer precise subjects and strong verbs: “We measured…” or “The assay detected…” over long nominalizations.
- Put given/contextual information before new/important information when it improves flow.
- Keep modifiers next to what they modify; make pronoun antecedents explicit.
- Use parallel grammar for compared groups, listed aims, and coordinated clauses.
- Split sentences carrying multiple claims, qualifications, and parenthetical details.
- Use consistent technical terms; stylistic synonym swapping can create false distinctions.
- Use signposting only when it expresses a real relation: contrast, cause, sequence, example, or inference.

## Tense, voice, and stance

Use present tense for accepted knowledge and what the paper/figure shows; past tense for completed procedures and observed results; present perfect when summarizing a continuing research record. Active voice is often clearer, but passive voice is useful when the procedure or object matters more than the actor. Follow field and journal conventions.

Calibrate verbs to evidence: `shows/demonstrates` for strong direct support; `is consistent with/suggests/may indicate` for limited inference. State limitations specifically rather than adding ritual caveats. Remove hype such as `groundbreaking`, `unprecedented`, and `perfect` unless a narrowly defined, verified claim justifies it.

## Numbers, units, and typography

Follow journal style and the current SI Brochure. Keep a space between a number and most unit symbols, use upright unit symbols, do not pluralize symbols, and define nonstandard abbreviations on first use. Keep decimal precision commensurate with measurement and uncertainty. Use an en dash or the journal's required style for ranges; do not use a hyphen as a minus sign.

## Final language audit

Check for changed meaning, dangling modifiers, ambiguous comparison, missing articles, singular/plural mismatch, subject-verb agreement, inconsistent hyphenation/capitalization, undefined abbreviations, wordy noun strings, mixed US/UK spelling, and conclusions stronger than the source text. Use the University of Manchester Academic Phrasebank only as a phrase-pattern resource; adapt language and never paste large passages or let stock phrases replace reasoning.
