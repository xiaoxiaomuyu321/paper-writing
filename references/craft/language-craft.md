# Language craft — distilled from the books

Authority on sentence, paragraph, and style technique for every journal. Each technique carries
**book anchors** (book + KB locator); use `scripts/paper_kb.py query "<keywords>" --source "<book>"`
to pull the original passage when you need the full wording or a worked example.
Sources: HTW = Gastel & Day 8e (2016); Tur = Turabian 7e (2003).

## 1. Front-load: familiar first, new last

- **State the main point, then the details.** International-journal prose is direct: topic sentence
  first, support after; the opposite (circling around the point) reads as evasive.
  *(HTW — CULTURAL DIFFERENCES TO CONSIDER)*
- **The first seven or eight words set the sentence.** Apply the seven principles as a checklist:
  no long introductory phrases (get to the subject fast), short concrete subjects, don't interrupt
  subject and verb, put key actions in verbs, old information before new, voice chosen to serve
  those rules, first person where warranted. Easy first words make readers read faster, understand
  better, and remember longer. *(Tur — Focus on the First Seven or Eight Words of a Sentence)*
- **Old-new flow controls voice, not habit.** Put given/contextual information at sentence start,
  new information at the end — and use passive *exactly* when active voice would force new material
  into the subject slot ("if you never make a verb passive, you'll write sentences that contradict
  the old-new principle"). Active remains the default: it is "usually more precise and less wordy."
  Name the agent, including "I/we" — "it was found that" hides the doer; "the authors found" is
  "pretentious, verbose, and imprecise." Passive is most tolerable in Methods.
  *(Tur — Choose Active or Passive Verbs; HTW — ACTIVE VERSUS PASSIVE VOICE)*

## 2. One idea per unit; verbs carry the work

- **Simple declarative sentences** (subject–verb–object) are the workhorse: "the sheer beauty of the
  simple declarative sentence" — they avoid most serious grammatical problems and serve non-native
  and busy readers alike. Split sentences carrying multiple claims or qualifications.
  *(HTW — KEEP IT SIMPLE)*
- **Turn nouns into verbs.** "Examination of the patients was carried out" → "I examined the
  patients"; "separation of the compounds was accomplished" → "the compounds were separated."
  Never introduce an action buried in an abstract noun with an empty verb (have/do/make/be).
  *(HTW — NOUN PROBLEMS; Tur — Put Key Actions in Verbs, Not in Nouns)*
- **Subjects name the main character** in a few concrete words; long abstract subjects ("a 14-word
  subject whose simple subject is 'adoption'") block comprehension. Keep subject and verb together —
  move long interrupters to the sentence's start or end (end if in doubt); if a sentence needs two
  comma-paired interruptions, rewrite it. *(Tur — Make Subjects Short and Concrete;
  Avoid Interrupting Subjects and Verbs; Other Uses)*
- **Dangling modifier rule:** the agent of the action must be the subject of the modified clause
  ("By filtering through Whatman no. 1 filter paper, Smith separated the components" is the error
  pattern). Noun-cluster adjectives get a hyphen to show which words function together.
  *(HTW — DANGLING MODIFIERS; NOUN PROBLEMS)*

## 3. Tense is epistemic, not decorative

- **Present tense = established (published) knowledge; past tense = your own (unpublished) work.**
  This is framed as ethics, not grammar: "Whenever you state previously published findings, ethics
  requires you to treat the work with respect. You do this by using the present tense." A published
  result later falsified moves to past. *(HTW — TENSE IN SCIENTIFIC WRITING)*
- **By section:** Abstract, Materials & Methods, Results in past; Introduction mixed (present for
  established knowledge, past for your paper's summary). Four standing exceptions: attribution
  ("Smith (1998) showed that streptomycin inhibits S. nocolor"), presentation ("Table 4 shows…"),
  statistical/calculation results (present), and general truths (present).
  *(HTW — TENSE IN SCIENTIFIC WRITING)*
- **Attribution verbs for a source's text stay in the present tense** — "writes, notes, claims,
  argues," not "wrote, claimed" — unless the discipline says otherwise.
  *(Tur — Run-in Quotations)*

## 4. Concision is a system, not an instinct

- **Jargon test:** write for the hundreds of people whose work is *slightly* related to yours, not
  the half-dozen doing exactly your work. Three jargon types: (1) confused unintelligible language
  — banned; (2) field terminology — fine if readers know it or you define it; (3) obscure
  pretentious circumlocution — banned. Jargon is defined by *behavior*: choosing the longer word;
  the challenge is to show how "at this point in time" says more than "now" (it doesn't). A listed
  offender is fine once, jargon when repeated — audit your draft for repetition.
  *(HTW — MUMBLESPEAK AND OTHER SINS; DEFINITION OF JARGON; SPECIAL CASES)*
- **Substitution discipline** (Appendix 2 pattern): "a considerable amount of" → much; "a majority
  of" → most; "a small number of" → a few; "absolutely essential" → essential; "accounted for by
  the fact" → because; "in this case" → here; "in most cases" → usually; "in all cases" → always;
  "in no cases" → never; prefer plain "about" over ~20 wordier substitutes.
  *(HTW — Words and Expressions to Avoid (Appendix 2); SPECIAL CASES)*
- **Big things have little names:** "Long words name little things. All big things have little
  names, such as life and death, peace and war." *(HTW — chapter epigraph)*
- **Kill the vague value words.** Claims built on *important / interesting / significant* are
  unfalsifiable: "Masks play a significant role in many religious ceremonies" must become "masks
  allow religious celebrants to bring deities to life so that worshipers experience them directly."
  A good claim makes a reader think "Really? How interesting," not "I know that."
  *(Tur — State and Evaluate Your Claim)*
- **Self-canceling and redundant pairs** get deleted: "well-seasoned novice," "young juveniles,"
  "authentic replicas," "viable alternative." Avoid double negatives ("not unimportant" →
  "important": "say what things are, not what they are not"), "and/or" (a "visual and mental
  monstrosity"), "It is / There is" openers ("It is not necessary to remove this structure" →
  "This structure need not be removed"). *(HTW — MISUSE OF WORDS; READABLE WRITING)*
- **Word-misuse traps with fixed remedies:** *amount* (mass/aggregate) vs *number* (countable);
  *like* is a preposition — use *as* as conjunction; *only* changes meaning by position — place it
  next to the word it limits; make every *it* antecedent explicit.
  *(HTW — MISUSE OF WORDS)*
- **The "Ten Commandments" core as a self-check pass:** pronoun-antecedent agreement, subject-verb
  agreement, no double negatives, no split infinitives, no clichés, no hyperbole.
  *(HTW — THE TEN COMMANDMENTS OF GOOD WRITING)*

## 5. Precision of claims (the books' hedging system)

The books give no word-list of hedges; they give a system — use all three layers:

1. **Scope claims to the data** (spotlight rule): "the best you can do is shine a spotlight on one
   area of the truth" — extrapolating beyond the data "may appear foolish" and undermines even the
   supported conclusions. State your part of the truth in the simplest words; "verbose language and
   fancy technical words are used to convey shallow thought." *(HTW — DEFINING SCIENTIFIC TRUTH)*
2. **Calibrate verbs to evidence strength:** present-tense for established knowledge, past for your
   own work, and match assertion strength to support (see §3). *(HTW — TENSE IN SCIENTIFIC WRITING)*
3. **State strengths and limitations yourself** — both are expected disclosure: naming substantial
   limitations and their likely impact shows the work is publishable; hiding them "runs counter to
   the openness that should characterize science." *(HTW — NOTING STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS)*
- **In argumentative prose, back claims with evidence, not logic alone:** principles (warrants)
  always have exceptions, so a claim that rests on "an unassailable principle" reads as ideological;
  supply the warrant explicitly *and* the evidence. State the warrant that connects a reason to the
  claim so the reader cannot ask "so what?" *(Tur — Distinguish Arguments Based on Evidence;
  Establish the Relevance of Your Reasons)*

## 6. International clarity (for every reader, not just natives)

- Use words with one or a few meanings, not many; **largely avoid idioms**; keep sentences simply
  structured; avoid literary, cultural, and sports allusions. *(HTW — SERVING INTERNATIONAL READERS)*
- **Retain optional clarifying words** that prevent misreading: "I believe that Professor Day knows
  …" not "I believe Professor Day knows …" (which can be misread as "I believe Professor Day").
  *(HTW — SERVING INTERNATIONAL READERS)*
- **Communicate the science, don't impress with English:** no TOEFL vocabulary, no display
  sentences; "the goal of a scientific paper is to communicate the science, not to impress readers
  with your English level." Draft in English and resist mid-draft polishing: "do not try to make
  the English perfect, as doing so can disrupt your flow of ideas."
  *(HTW — MORE STRATEGIES FOR ENGLISH-LANGUAGE WRITING)*
- **Build section word/phrase lists from the target journal's recent papers** (including common
  prepositional phrases) and consult them while writing. *(HTW — MORE STRATEGIES FOR
  ENGLISH-LANGUAGE WRITING)*
- Metaphor policy: avoid mostly; if used, keep them fresh (stale imagery dulls; mixed metaphors
  "mix comprehension"); watch words locked to one partner ("leap" → only "quantum leap"). No
  euphemisms: laboratory animals are not "sacrificed," "they are killed and that's that."
  *(HTW — METAPHORICALLY SPEAKING; EUPHEMISMS)*

## 7. Revision is the writing

- **"Good writing tends to be largely a matter of good revising."** Expect rounds — a journal
  editor: "If I'm lucky, only about 10 times." Self-revise first, then show it to others, then
  revise again. *(HTW — REVISING YOUR WORK)*
- **Revise top-down** (Tur's order): "first the 'outer frame' (introduction and conclusion), then
  overall organization, then sections, paragraphs, sentences, finally stylistic issues such as
  spelling and punctuation" — because "we all know our own work too well to read it as others will."
  HTW notes two defensible pass orders (big-scale first, or language first so you can see the piece
  clearly); pick one and hold it. *(Tur — Revising Your Draft; HTW — PREPARING TO EDIT YOUR WORK)*
- **The 11-question self-audit:** all information it should include? anything to delete? all
  accurate? reasoning sound? content consistent? logically organized? clearly worded? concise
  ("stated your points briefly, simply, and directly")? grammar/spelling/punctuation/word use
  correct? figures/tables well designed? compliant with the journal's instructions?
  *(HTW — REVISING YOUR WORK)*
- **The 8 Cs pass** (run in sequence; "writing that achieves all eight Cs is likely to excel at
  communication"): **C**ompliance (instructions, field conventions, human/animal requirements) ·
  **C**ompleteness (every component has all information it should) · **C**omposition (structure
  appropriate; paragraphs well structured with strong topic sentences; one idea leads smoothly to
  the next) · **C**orrectness (information, logic, grammar, spelling, punctuation, word use — if
  verb tenses are weak, do a dedicated extra tense pass) · **C**larity (unclear words defined,
  abbreviations defined at first use, *it* antecedents checked, transitional words added where a
  real relation exists — also/first/then/however — reasoning made explicit, long sentences split) ·
  **C**onsistency (numbers match between table and text, abstract matches body, terminology and
  formatting consistent, items in consistent order) · **C**onciseness (long words → short
  equivalents, wordy phrases condensed, redundancies removed, tangents deleted — "take care not to
  decrease clarity or diminish meaning") · **C**ourtesy (neutral language about others' work:
  "Previous researchers have failed to explore" → "Previous researchers did not explore"; inclusive
  phrasing for population groups). *(HTW — ITEMS TO NOTICE: 8 Cs)*
- **Gain distance before editing:** set the draft aside for hours; change its appearance — print it,
  change typeface or margins, print on colored paper — "to aid in encountering the writing afresh."
  Read the draft aloud to catch missing words and awkwardness. *(HTW — PREPARING TO EDIT YOUR WORK)*
- **Let the draft cool, then paraphrase it** (final-pass device): restating your own passages in
  fresh words exposes where the writing still sounds borrowed or flat. *(Tur — Revising Your Draft)*

## 8. Proofreading mechanics

- **Comprehension-reading misses ~90% of errors** (though it catches omissions — dropped lines).
  For the rest: "you must slowly examine each word." Read the proof **backward, from last word to
  first** to kill comprehension; or pair up (one reads aloud, the other follows the manuscript).
  *(HTW — MISSPELLED WORDS)*
- **Numbers: examine each and every one** — keyboarding errors cluster in tables, "you are the only
  person who can catch such errors," and "a misplaced decimal point can sometimes make a published
  paper almost useless." *(HTW — MISSPELLED WORDS)*
- **Never delegate proofreading entirely** — a colleague who outsourced it to an office worker found
  the article title misspelled only after publication. *(HTW — MISSPELLED WORDS)*
- **Spell-checkers recognize misspellings, not real-word errors** ("pubic" instead of "public");
  keep a custom dictionary for technical terms and still proofread. "A spell-checker is not a
  substitute for a good dictionary or careful proofreading." *(HTW — CHECKING YOUR MANUSCRIPT;
  Tur — chapter 10 spelling pass)*
- **Proofs exist to verify type composition** — editorial input and typesetting introduce *new*
  errors (Greek letters become squiggles, lines get cut): "it is only the printed version in the
  journal that counts." *(HTW — WHY PROOFS ARE SENT TO AUTHORS)*
- **Line-break pass (printed/PDF output):** never break within numerals, numeral-plus-symbol terms,
  or a date's day/month; don't break abbreviations except at an existing hyphen; never begin a line
  with a closing mark (quotation/parenthesis/bracket) or end one with an opening mark; don't break
  an ellipsis (use the ellipsis character); break URLs only after a colon, slash, or @ and before a
  period. *(Tur — Breaks over Spaces and Punctuation)*
- **Read at least one late version on paper**, "as your readers will" — screen editing is less
  reliable. *(Tur — Revising Your Draft)*
- **Make the editor an instructor:** notice every editorial revision, ask why recurrent changes
  were made, compile a master list of others' fixes, and update your own checklists with the
  insights. Get fresh-eyes review from someone who has not seen the manuscript.
  *(HTW — Specialized Checklist; CHECKING YOUR MANUSCRIPT)*

## 9. Anti-patterns quick list (from both books)

Clichéd openers (repeating the assignment's language, "Webster defines X as…," "For centuries,
scholars have debated…") — replace with a striking quotation, a striking fact, or a relevant
anecdote *(Tur — Draft a New First Sentence)* · dictionary definitions as openings *(Tur)* ·
overclaiming beyond the data *(HTW — DEFINING SCIENTIFIC TRUTH)* · euphemism, double negatives,
"and/or," nominalized verbs, "It is/There is" openers, hedge-free absolutes
*(HTW — MISUSE OF WORDS; READABLE WRITING)* · patchwork writing stitched from others' material —
"You must balance quotations, paraphrases, and summaries with your own fresh ideas"
*(Tur — Quote, Paraphrase, and Summarize Appropriately)* · "first / unique / unprecedented"
without a narrowly defined verified claim *(both books' claim discipline)*.
