# Argument craft — distilled from Turabian (and HTW)

How to build the research argument: question → hypothesis → claim → evidence → organization.
Condensed from Turabian 7e (2003), with Gastel & Day 8e (HTW) where it adds. Each rule carries
**book anchors** (KB locator); query
`scripts/paper_kb.py query "<keywords>" --source "A Manual for Writers"` for original passages.
Anchors: Tur = Turabian 7e · HTW = Gastel & Day 8e.

## 1. The question: start here, test it hard

- **Frame the project as topic → question → significance** (the "So what?" formula): "I am
  working on the topic of X, because I want to find out how/why/whether Y — *so what if you
  do?* — so that I can help others understand how/why/whether Z." Draft the project in these
  three numbered lines before researching; the "so what" slot forces the significance.
  *(Tur — Three Kinds of Questions That Researchers Ask)*
- **Know which kind of question you're asking:** conceptual (what readers should *understand* —
  most common in academic work) vs practical (what to *do* — dominates professional work);
  conceptual answers "often turn out to be unexpectedly relevant to solving a practical
  problem." *(Tur — Three Kinds of Questions That Researchers Ask)*
- **Scrap a question that fails any of the three tests:** (1) you can answer it too easily
  (look it up / summarize one source); (2) you can't find good evidence (no facts, taste-based,
  too broad — "plow through countless reports"); (3) you can't plausibly disprove the answer —
  "If you can't imagine disproving a claim, then proving it is pointless."
  *(Tur — Evaluate Your Questions)*
- **If no answer exists, the project can still succeed** by arguing for the question — showing
  why it matters and what a good answer would look like, or showing that a widely accepted
  answer is wrong. "It's as important to ask a new question as it is to answer an old one."
  *(Tur — If You Can't Find an Answer, Argue for Your Question)*
- **HTW's companion move (journal papers): choose the target journal early — before the writing
  begins** — so the paper is "geared appropriately to the audience" from the first draft; a poor
  match ends in desk returns, delayed publication, or being "buried in inappropriate journals."
  *(HTW — WHY DECIDE EARLY, WHY DECIDE WELL)*

## 2. The working hypothesis: hold it lightly, use it precisely

- **The research stage sequence:** question → tentative answer (working hypothesis) → claim.
  "Your claim is the center of your argument, the point of your report" (what many teachers call
  the thesis). Keep the hypothesis tentative while gathering evidence; promote it to a claim
  only when the report will "back [it] up with good reasons and evidence."
  *(Tur — Turn Your Working Hypothesis into a Claim)*
- **Don't fear an early working hypothesis** — researchers who don't state one "usually have one
  in mind"; "it's better to start with a flawed hypothesis than with none at all." The risk is
  loving it: "don't fall too hard for your first hypothesis: the more you like it, the less
  easily you'll see its flaws." *(Tur — Beware the Risks in a Working Hypothesis)*
- **Let the hypothesis specify the evidence:** it tells you what data to watch for (numbers?
  quotations? observations?) and — more important — **what kind of evidence would disprove
  it**. "Until you have a hypothesis, you can't know whether any data you collect are
  relevant." *(Tur — Decide on a Working Hypothesis)*
- **A hypothesis stall is a diagnostic on the question**, not on you: if you can't imagine any
  working hypothesis, review your exploratory questions or restart the topic — "That costs time
  in the short run, but it may save you from a failed project." *(Tur — Decide on a Working
  Hypothesis)*
- **Never defer the answer to the last page:** "writing, even revising, is itself an act of
  discovery," so the best answer may firm up only at the end — but don't get deeply into the
  project without "at least the glimmer of a possible answer." *(Tur — Decide on a Working
  Hypothesis)*
- **Test every claim by hunting for doubt:** get into the habit of asking "What could cast
  doubt on my claim?"; for a thesis or dissertation you must know the issues your field is
  likely to raise. Even going through the motions prevents "jumping to questionable
  conclusions." *(Tur — Acknowledge and Respond to Readers' Points of View)*
- **Disagreement is the seed of the hypothesis:** don't just note that a source's views
  contradict yours — "use that disagreement to encourage your own productive thinking"; name
  the contradiction's kind (of kind, part-whole, developmental/historical) and let it generate
  your question. *(Tur — Look for Creative Disagreement)*

## 3. The claim: state it, size it, place it

- **Ban vague value words in the claim sentence:** claims built on *important / interesting /
  significant* are unfalsifiable — "Masks play a significant role in many religious
  ceremonies" must become "masks allow religious celebrants to bring deities to life so that
  worshipers experience them directly." A good claim makes a reader think "Really? How
  interesting," not "I know that." *(Tur — State and Evaluate Your Claim)*
- **State the claim at the end of the introduction** so readers know where you're being taken;
  **restate it, more specifically, at the top of the conclusion**. The fear that early claims
  bore readers is baseless: "If you ask an interesting question, readers will want to see how
  well you can support its answer." *(Tur — Sketch a Working Introduction)*
- **Make three boundaries unmissable:** readers must recognize quickly and unambiguously where
  the introduction ends, where the conclusion begins, and which sentences state the claim —
  insert a subhead or extra space at both boundaries and signal the claim clearly.
  *(Tur — Check Your Introduction, Conclusion, and Claim)*
- **Support claims with evidence, not logic alone:** inferences from general principles
  (warrants) read as ideological because principles always have exceptions — "support your
  claims with as much evidence as you can, even when you think you have the power of logic on
  your side"; watch for warrants "true always and without exception" — a reader will test them
  for exceptions. *(Tur — Distinguish Arguments Based on Evidence)*
- **State the warrant explicitly** — the general principle showing how a reason is relevant to
  the claim — to preempt the "so what?" objection ("When a valued object wasn't mentioned in
  early nineteenth-century wills, it usually wasn't part of the estate.").
  *(Tur — Establish the Relevance of Your Reasons)*
- **HTW's claim discipline (scientific papers):** claim only what the data illuminate — "the
  best you can do is shine a spotlight on one area of the truth"; extrapolating beyond the data
  "may appear foolish" and undermines even the supported conclusions. Calibrate verbs to
  evidence strength and disclose limitations yourself. *(HTW — DEFINING SCIENTIFIC TRUTH;
  NOTING STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS)*
- **Match argument order to audience agreement:** if the audience largely agrees, present the
  main point early then support it; if they may oppose, "starting with mutually supported ideas
  and relatively unexceptionable data and then showing how they lead to your conclusions"
  gains the most agreement — and include arguments for and against your point of view.
  *(HTW — EDITORIALS)*

## 4. Sources and notes: engage, don't mine

- **Skim to filter, read to engage.** Evaluate the many sources you find by skimming quickly
  for exactly two criteria — relevance and reliability; deep reading comes only for the
  survivors. "Note-taking is not clerical work": don't read a source "mechanically, just mining
  it for data" — engage "its implications, consequences, shortcomings, and new possibilities,"
  "as if its writer were sitting with you, eager for a conversation."
  *(Tur — Evaluate Sources for Relevance and Reliability; Engaging Sources)*
- **Match sources to the three tiers readers expect:** primary sources for evidence, secondary
  sources to learn from other researchers, tertiary sources for introductory overviews — list
  which tier the project needs before searching. *(Tur — Understanding Useful Sources)*
- **Finding and reading are one loop:** "Once you have a promising source, read it to find other
  sources"; expect the notes to generate gaps and new questions that only more sources fill —
  research happens "repeatedly and simultaneously." *(Tur — Finding Useful Sources)*
- **Use a note template that forces your own thinking:** per source — bibliographic data,
  keywords, labeled places for Claim/Data/Your questions, and a section **dedicated to your own
  responses, agreements, disagreements, and speculations**; start a new page per general idea
  so keywords let you "sort and re-sort your notes by subject matter" later.
  *(Tur — Create Templates for Notes)*
- **Double-safeguard borrowed material at capture time** (the upstream anti-plagiarism rule):
  quoted words in a distinctive color/font inside large quotation marks; paraphrases in a
  distinctive color inside curly brackets — so both survive even if the file loses its
  formatting. For multi-line quotations, download or photocopy the page (source name + keywords
  added) rather than retyping. *(Tur — Create Templates for Notes; Guard against Inadvertent
  Plagiarism)*
- **Note against the hypothesis — including the data that hurts it:** record supporting
  reasons, supporting evidence, and "views that undermine or even contradict your hypothesis";
  "Do not limit your notes to supporting data" — you'll need the contradicting material when
  you make your case. Also keep context, definitions, analogies, and "strikingly original
  language." *(Tur — Take Notes Relevant to Your Question and Working Hypothesis)*
- **HTW's capture rule:** "be sure to include information about the source when you copy or
  download materials others have written" — track sources at the point of capture, not at
  writing time. *(HTW — CREDIT)*

## 5. Planning and organizing the argument

- **Storyboard, not outline, for long work:** an outline "can force you to specify too much too
  soon and so lock up a final form before you've done your best thinking"; a storyboard is
  "like an outline spread over several pages, with lots of space for adding data and ideas as
  you go" — it can "help you plan your search for evidence, organize your argument, write a
  first draft, and test a final one." Reserve sketch outlines for short papers.
  *(Tur — Build a Storyboard to Plan and Guide Your Work)*
- **Make the whole plan visible at a glance:** spread storyboard pages across a wall, group
  related pages, minor sections below major ones — "a 'picture' of your project that shows you
  at a glance the design of the whole and your progress through it"; pages move physically when
  you try a new organization. *(Tur — Build a Storyboard to Plan and Guide Your Work)*
- **Compress the argument into the elevator story:** one three-part sentence — "I'm working on
  the topic of X, because I want to find out Y, so that I (and you) can better understand Z" —
  grown into a short story that adds the working hypothesis and the major supporting reasons;
  use it to open writing-group sessions and as a surrogate-reader test: "If your group has a
  problem with your draft, so will your final readers." *(Tur — Organize a Writing Support
  Group)*
- **The introduction's three aims, sized at ~10%:** put the research in the context of other
  research, make readers understand why they should read the report, and give them a framework
  for understanding it. "Most introductions run about 10 percent of the whole (in the sciences
  they are often shorter)"; in theses/dissertations introduction and conclusion are usually
  separate chapters. *(Tur — Writing Your Final Introduction and Conclusion)*
- **Write the final introduction and conclusion after the final draft** — "Once you have a
  final draft and can see what you have actually written, you can write your final
  introduction and conclusion"; the framing parts "crucially influence how readers will read
  and remember the rest of it." Draft a working introduction early; rewrite the final one
  last. Then **write the title last** — when the argument's center of gravity is settled.
  Order: final introduction → final conclusion → title.
  *(Tur — Writing Your Final Introduction and Conclusion; Write Your Title Last)*
- **HTW's funnel (journal introduction):** move "from broad and general to narrow and
  specific" through the five moves — (1) nature and scope of the problem with all possible
  clarity, (2) brief literature review orienting the reader + the gap, (3) objective/hypothesis,
  (4) method of investigation (+ why, if needed), (5) sometimes principal results/conclusions —
  so the introduction "comfortably funnel[s] readers into reading about the details of your
  research." Most of it in present tense (established knowledge).
  *(HTW — GUIDELINES (introduction chapter))*
- **Persuasion ordering for contested claims:** present the main point early when the audience
  agrees; when it may oppose, lead with mutually supported ideas and unexceptionable data,
  then show how they lead to your conclusions; include the case against your view.
  *(HTW — EDITORIALS)*

## 6. Drafting and revising the argument

- **Write the first draft for yourself:** "They write a first draft not for their readers, but
  for themselves, to see whether they can make the case they hoped to (or a better one)" — then
  revise until it meets the readers' needs. The error is thinking a draft means being done.
  *(Tur — Revising Your Draft)*
- **Revise top-down:** outer frame (introduction and conclusion) → overall organization →
  sections → paragraphs → sentences → finally style (spelling, punctuation). "You're likely to
  make the best revisions if you revise from whole to part, even if at the moment you're
  revising, apart is the only whole you have." *(Tur — Revising Your Draft)*
- **Revise only as objectively as a reader:** "we all know our own work too well to read it as
  others will" — make the mechanical steps explicit so you "can analyze your draft objectively"
  instead of reading "what you want your readers to get out of it."
  *(Tur — Revising Your Draft)*
- **Match drafting to temperament, use placeholders:** "if you draft slowly, plan carefully…
  if you draft quickly, start early" (fast drafters who skip ahead and omit quotations or
  statistics "need lots of time to revise"). When a stylistic issue blocks you, "insert a [?]
  and keep on writing until they run out of gas, then go back and fix it."
  *(Tur — Draft in the Way That Feels Most Comfortable)*
- **Let the draft cool, then paraphrase it:** "What seems good one day often looks different the
  next." On return, don't read straight through — skim the top-level parts (introduction, first
  paragraph of each major section, conclusion); then paraphrase the argument for someone who
  hasn't read it: does the paraphrase hang together? does it fairly sum up the argument?
  *(Tur — Let Your Draft Cool, Then Paraphrase It)*
- **The surrogate-reader comprehension test:** ask someone to read *only* the introduction and
  the introductions of major sections — "how well that person summarizes your report will
  predict how well your readers will understand it." *(Tur — Let Your Draft Cool, Then
  Paraphrase It)*
- **HTW's revision system (run after the argument is settled):** expect many rounds ("If
  I'm lucky, only about 10 times"); the 11-question self-audit; the 8 Cs pass; distance
  techniques (set aside hours, print it, change typeface, read aloud); proofreading mechanics
  (numbers one by one, word-by-word or backward, never outsourced wholesale).
  *(HTW — REVISING YOUR WORK; PREPARING TO EDIT YOUR WORK; ITEMS TO NOTICE: 8 Cs;
  MISSPELLED WORDS — full text in `language-craft.md` §7–8)*

## 7. Counterargument: acknowledge and compare, don't rebut

- **"You will lose readers if you argue that your view is right and theirs is wrong."** Don't
  treat differing views as mere objections — "acknowledge the differences, then compare them so
  that readers can understand your argument on its own terms"; even readers who still disagree
  will respect that you understood their views. *(Tur — Acknowledge and Respond to Readers'
  Points of View)*
- **Plant objections where readers will actually raise them:** anticipate the plausible
  questions and objections, and add the acknowledgments and responses to your plan (storyboard)
  at the point where readers are likely to object — for a thesis or dissertation you must know
  "the issues that others in your field are likely to raise." *(Tur — same section)*
- **Qualifying and contradicting data are case-material, not junk:** "You will need to respond
  to data that qualify or even contradict your hypothesis when you make your case in support of
  it" — keep a standing note category for undermining views so the response is built in, not
  bolted on. *(Tur — Take Notes Relevant to Your Question and Working Hypothesis)*
- **Ideology tells:** arguments resting on "unassailable principles" ignore complications "because
  they are convinced that their principles must be right, regardless of evidence to the
  contrary" — "Such arguments are more ideological than factual." Anchor every inference in
  evidence, not just in the warrant. *(Tur — Distinguish Arguments Based on Evidence from
  Arguments Based on Warrants)*
- **HTW's neutral-gap rule applies here too:** describe others' omissions without accusation —
  "Smith (2015) did not study…" is fine; "totally overlooked / ignored / failed to" is not.
  *(HTW — CITATIONS IN THE TEXT)*

## 8. Thesis/dissertation specifics (long work)

- Introduction and conclusion are usually **separate chapters**; the introduction targets ~10%
  of the whole; when the introduction is clearly distinct, label its first page "Introduction"
  (arabic numbering begins with the first page of the text). *(Tur — Writing Your Final
  Introduction and Conclusion; Text (Appendix))*
- You may take longer to firm up the hypothesis, but never start without "at least the glimmer
  of a possible answer"; the claim is restated more specifically in the conclusion chapter.
  *(Tur — Decide on a Working Hypothesis; Sketch a Working Introduction)*
- **Divided texts get part-title pages:** when the text divides into parts of two or more
  chapters each, begin each part with a part-title page (the first follows the introduction,
  "even if the introduction is labeled chapter 1"). *(Tur — Text (Appendix))*
- **Department conventions override the book:** thesis/dissertation format and submission
  requirements "are set by your department or your university's office of theses and
  dissertations," and most universities' own requirements "take precedence over the guidelines
  suggested here" — review them before submitting. *(Tur — Appendix (Paper Format and
  Submission))*

  `../thesis-and-dissertation.md` (contract level) — this file supplies the argument craft.
