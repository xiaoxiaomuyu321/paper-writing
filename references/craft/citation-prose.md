# Citation in prose — distilled from the books

Authority on integrating sources into prose, paraphrase discipline, plagiarism avoidance, and
Chicago citation mechanics in text. Each technique carries **book anchors** (book + KB locator);
query `scripts/paper_kb.py query "<keywords>" --source "A Manual for Writers"` for the original
passage or worked example. Sources: Tur = Turabian 7e (2003); HTW = Gastel & Day 8e (2016).

## 1. Choose the form by argumentative role, not habit

Summarize / paraphrase / quote — pick by what the material does in your argument *(Tur — Know When
to Summarize, Paraphrase, or Quote)*:

- **Summarize** when you need only the general point (of a passage, section, or whole work) or the
  source is not important enough for more.
- **Paraphrase** when you can state the source's idea more clearly or concisely, or details matter
  but the exact words don't.
- **Quote** when: the words themselves are the evidence; a view you disagree with must be stated
  exactly for fairness; the source is an authority backing your view; the wording is strikingly
  original; or the words frame your discussion.
- **Strength ladder:** "A paraphrase is never as good evidence as a direct quotation" — when the
  claim depends on exact words (e.g., how different outlets phrased the same event), quote.
- **Field norm:** humanities quote most; social and natural scientists typically paraphrase and
  summarize. In scientific papers "rather, authors paraphrase what others have said"; quoting is
  justified only in rare cases — e.g., "when an author has phrased a concept extraordinarily well."
  *(HTW — CREDIT)*
- **Balance:** most of the work must be your own words reflecting your own thinking: "Do not merely
  repeat, or worse, download, words and ideas of others that you stitch together with a few
  sentences of your own." *(Tur — Quote, Paraphrase, and Summarize Appropriately)*
- **Every figure or table gets a framing sentence** that states the claim the representation
  supports; when in doubt, have someone unfamiliar with the data judge it "for impact and
  clarity." *(Tur — Follow Guidelines for Tables, Bar Charts, and Line Graphs)*

## 2. Paraphrase that survives scrutiny

- **The test:** fair paraphrase represents the idea *more clearly or pointedly* than the source
  does; it "cross[es] the line from fair paraphrase to plagiarism if [readers] can match your words
  and phrasing with those of your source." Tur shows three tiers on the same two sentences —
  plagiaristic / borderline / clean — as a calibration exercise.
  *(Tur — Don't Paraphrase Too Closely)*
- **The look-away procedure:** "Read the passage, look away, think about it for a moment; then
  still looking away, paraphrase it in your own words." Then finger-check: can you run your finger
  along your sentence and find the same ideas in the same order in the source? "If you can, so can
  your readers. Try again." *(Tur — Don't Paraphrase Too Closely)*
- **HTW's stricter scientific standard:** paraphrasing "entails truly presenting the ideas in one's
  own way; changing a word or two does not constitute paraphrasing." *(HTW — CREDIT)*
- **Draft blind, then check** (anti-parroting workflow): "consider drafting paragraphs without
  looking directly at the source materials; then look at the materials to check for accuracy."
  *(HTW — CREDIT)*
- **Record exact words at capture time:** if you don't record important words exactly now you can't
  quote them later; copy or photocopy passages more often than you think you'll need.
  *(Tur — Know When to Summarize, Paraphrase, or Quote)*

## 3. Quotation integration

- **Run-in (≤4 lines) vs block (≥5 lines)** threshold, double quotation marks for run-in.
  Three signal patterns for run-in quotes *(Tur — Run-in Quotations)*:
  1. attribution phrase — "Ricoeur writes, 'The boundary between plot and argument is no easier to trace.'"
  2. woven with *that*, no comma — "Ricoeur warns us that 'the boundary…'"
  3. attribution mid-quote, set off by commas — "'The boundary between plot and argument,' says Ricoeur, 'is no easier to trace.'"
- **Never open with bare says/states/claims** — add interpretation: "Diamond suggests that one
  lesson we can learn from the past is not to expect history to repeat itself. 'The histories…'"
  Supply the reading before the quote does the work. *(Tur — Integrate Quotations into Your Text)*
- **Weave the quote's grammar into your sentence** — modify the quoted fragment (brackets for
  added/changed words, ellipses for deletions) without changing its meaning, so it becomes part of
  your syntax rather than a bolted-on string. *(Tur — Integrate Quotations into Your Text)*
- **Block-quote punctuation:** complete introducing sentence → colon; attribution phrase + name →
  comma; woven into your syntax → no punctuation if none would ordinarily appear there. Block:
  indented like a paragraph's first line, single-spaced, blank line before and after, **no**
  quotation marks added at beginning/end (preserve any inside the original).
  *(Tur — Block Quotations)*
- **Author naming in prose:** full name on first reference (no Mr./Mrs./Ms./Professor), last name
  only thereafter, **never first names** ("Never this: …Steven goes on to claim that").
  *(Tur — Integrate Quotations into Your Text)*
- **Ellipses preserve meaning:** when trimming, audit the omission for negations and hedges —
  "preserve words that might change the entire meaning of the quotation (such as not, never, or
  always)" ("The change was sure to be beneficial once the immediate troubles subsided" must not
  become "The change was sure to be beneficial."). Mechanics: three spaced periods, inside the
  quotation marks/block, space before the first dot and after the last; no ellipsis before/after a
  clearly incomplete fragment, none at the quotation's start/end. *(Tur — Omissions)*
- **Brackets change words, never meaning** — visibly mark every addition or change.
  *(Tur — Integrate Quotations into Your Text)*
- **Quoting fragments:** mark only the striking phrases showing "originality of thought or
  expression"; ordinary technical phrasing passes into your voice. It's a gray area — "words that
  seem striking to some readers are commonplace to others." Once striking words are cited once, you
  may reuse them in your own phrasing without further marks ("begets more technology" → "one
  invention begets another"). *(Tur — Signal Every Quotation, Even When You Cite Its Source)*

## 4. Plagiarism is perceived, not intended

- **"We read words, not minds."** "Think of plagiarism not as an intended act but as a perceived
  one" — audit from the reader's side: does anything let a reader think you're passing off another
  writer's work? *(Tur — Don't Plead Ignorance, Misunderstanding, or Innocent Intentions)*
- **The implicit promise:** by signing, "you implicitly promise that you wrote every word that you
  don't clearly and specifically attribute to someone else" — including paraphrases, summaries,
  and general ideas or methods. *(Tur — same section)*
- **The author-recognition test** (the book's single best practical check): "If the person whose
  work you used read your report, would she recognize any of it as hers, including paraphrases and
  summaries, or even general ideas or methods?" If yes — cite, and mark exact-word sequences.
  *(Tur — same section)*
- **The three inadvertent risks:** (1) cited but used exact words without quotation marks/block;
  (2) paraphrased and cited, but in words "so similar to those of your source that they are almost
  a quotation"; (3) used ideas or methods from a source without citing it.
  *(Tur — Guard against Inadvertent Plagiarism)*
- **Two separate duties:** citation AND quotation marking — "you still risk a charge of plagiarism
  if you use the exact words of the source but fail to identify them as a quotation."
  *(Tur — Quoting Accurately and Avoiding Plagiarism)*
- **When a citation is always required:** exact words; paraphrase of ideas associated with a
  specific source (even with zero shared words); any idea, data, or method attributable to a source
  consulted. Optionally cite relevant-but-unquoted sources to show familiarity with claims at odds
  with your own. *(Tur — Situations Requiring Citations)*
- **Free/public online material still must be cited:** "Nothing releases you from the duty to
  acknowledge your use of anything you did not personally create yourself."
  *(Tur — Guard against Inadvertent Plagiarism)*
- **Notes that prevent plagiarism upstream:** (1) unambiguously identify borrowed words/ideas at
  capture — quotation marks *and* a distinguishing font for borrowed material; (2) never paraphrase
  so closely a reader can match phrasing and sense; for multi-line quotations, download or
  photocopy the page (source name + keywords added) rather than retyping.
  *(Tur — Guard against Inadvertent Plagiarism)*
- **Citing is not enough, citing what you haven't seen is worse:** "Responsible researchers avoid
  repeating quotations that they have not actually seen in the original." If the original is
  unavailable, mark it "quoted in" the secondary source (note: original entry + "quoted in…";
  reference list: original entry then "Quoted in …"; parenthetical: give only the original author,
  "(Zukofsky 1931, 269)"). "Secondhand reports of data have a high error rate" — verify important
  quotations, facts, and numbers against the primary in advanced work.
  *(Tur — One Source Quoted in Another; Read Secondary Sources)*
- **HTW's credit rule (scientific papers):** others' information/ideas must be cited; others'
  wording goes in quotation marks (or indented if long) with a reference. When unsure whether a
  string of words needs marks, add them — "an editor at the journal can easily remove them."
  Track sources at the point of copying or downloading: "be sure to include information about the
  source when you copy or download materials others have written." *(HTW — CREDIT)*
- **Accurate quotation = reliable edition + exact transcription + accurate source record** so
  readers can consult it themselves. "If you misquote, you fatally undermine your credibility, so
  double check your quote against the original. Then check it again."
  *(Tur — Quoting Accurately and Avoiding Plagiarism; Know When to Summarize, Paraphrase, or Quote)*
- **What must be cited is evidence, not logic:** "We think up logical reasons, but we collect hard
  evidence… you must always cite its source, even if you found it through your own observation or
  experiment; then you must show what you did to find it." Borrowing a secondary source's general
  logic needs no citation, but citing it "may give your own approach more authority."
  *(Tur — Support Your Claim with Reasons and Evidence; Read Secondary Sources)*

## 5. Chicago mechanics in prose

**Notes-bibliography (footnote/endnote) system** *(Tur — Referencing Notes in Text)*:
- Superscript number immediately after quoted material (run-in or block); for general citations, at
  the end of the sentence/clause, after terminal punctuation, quotation mark, or closing
  parenthesis. If the note attaches to material before a dash, number **before** the dash
  ("The bias surfaced in the Shotwell series⁴—though not obviously.").
- One reference number per location — never stack (5, 6); consolidate into a single note.
- No note numbers inside or at the end of titles, subtitles, or subheads: chapter-wide note →
  unnumbered footnote on the first page; section note → number after the section's first sentence.
- Notes give author names in standard order (first name first) with page numbers for specific
  passages; bibliography entries use inverted order (last name first).
  *(Tur — Order of Elements)*

**Author-date (reference list) system** *(Tur — Parenthetical Citations; Special Elements and
Format Issues)*:
- Parenthetical = author, date, (page for a specific passage), placed next to your reference to the
  source: "According to one scholar, 'The railroads had made Chicago…' (Cronon 1991, 92–93)."
- The name and date must **match the reference-list entry exactly**.
- Punctuation differs between the two places: list entries separate elements with periods;
  parentheses use no mark between author and date and a comma before the page
  — "(Smith 2001, 140)" not "Smith 2001, 140." with list punctuation.
- **Narrative citation:** author named in the sentence → drop the name from the parenthesis, keep
  date (and page): "Chang then describes the occupation of Nanking in great detail (1997, 159–67)."
- **Multiple works, one point:** one grouped parenthetical, semicolon-separated, ordered
  alphabetically, chronologically, or by importance per context
  ("(Armstrong and Malacinski 1989; Pickett and White 1995; Beigl 2004)").
- **Collisions:** same last name → first initial in every citation even if dates differ; initial
  clash → spell out first names (J. Smith 2001 vs T. Smith 1998). Same author + year → alphabetize
  by title in the list and suffix the year a, b, c in list and citation (Davis 1983a). No author →
  shortened title of up to four distinctive words. No date → n.d. in both places.
- **Never mix the two Chicago systems** — "do not confuse or combine the two styles."

**Bibliography / reference list** *(Tur — Arrangement of Entries; Types of Bibliographies;
Chapters and Other Titled Parts of a Book; Author's Name; A Word on Citation Software)*:
- One alphabetical list by the last name of whoever is first in each entry; hard return after each
  entry (check institutional letter-by-letter vs word-by-word rules for theses).
- Multiple works by one author: alphabetical by title (ignoring a/the), all solo works before
  coauthored ones; replace the name after the first entry with a 3-em dash ("———, ed.…").
- Includes every work cited in the text, plus optionally works that mattered to your thinking
  (label the fuller list "Bibliography" or "Sources Consulted"). An annotated bibliography
  annotates all entries or the most important ones; brief annotations in brackets after the
  publication data, no period after the brackets.
- **Book vs chapter:** cite the main title of any book offering a single continuous argument, even
  if you used only a section; for edited collections of independent pieces, cite the chapter or
  part ("By doing so, you help readers see how the source fits into your project"). Titled parts of
  single-author books: part title in quotes, then "In" + book title, with the part's full page span.
- Alphabetize particles by the letters actually present (de, di, D', van, Mac/Mc, al-, el-,
  Spanish y-compounds under the first part) — do not group similar particles.
- Citation software "is not a substitute for knowing the underlying principles of the style" —
  always review generated citations for accuracy and completeness.

## 6. Gaps to cover from current policy (time-sensitive)

The books predate modern conventions: AI-generated-text disclosure, data-availability statements,
and current OA/APC practice are **not** in these editions (HTW 2016 / Tur 2003). Apply the target
journal's current instructions for those; the books' general rules (cite evidence, mark exact
words, be transparent) still govern the underlying conduct.
