# Journal selection, submission, and peer review

## Select and verify a journal

Build a shortlist from journals that publish the same article type and topic. Compare scope fit, audience, recent relevant papers, article types, review model, turnaround evidence (if official), indexing, access model, fees/waivers, data/code and preprint policies, copyright/licence, length/format limits, transfer options, and conflicts with funder mandates.

Use official journal and publisher pages, not an invitation email. Apply Think. Check. Submit.; verify editorial-board identity/contact details, peer-review description, fees, archiving, persistent identifiers, and publisher memberships/claims. Do not use a single blacklist/whitelist, impact factor, acceptance-rate claim, or unsolicited invitation as the sole decision rule. Present fit and risk separately from prestige.

## Prepare the submission package

Read the current article-type instructions and convert each requirement into a ledger row. Typical components are manuscript, anonymized version if needed, title page, highlights/graphical abstract, figures, source data, tables, supplements, reporting checklist, cover letter, author/contribution/funding/conflict/ethics/data/code/AI statements, related manuscripts, permissions, and suggested/opposed reviewers where allowed.

The cover letter should identify the manuscript and article type, state the question, main supported contribution, fit with the journal, and required declarations. Do not repeat the abstract, exaggerate novelty, name competitors as opposed reviewers without a legitimate conflict, or claim exclusivity without checking all related submissions.

## Editorial and peer-review outcomes

For desk rejection, separate scope/priority/format reasons from scientific defects; improve or retarget rather than cosmetically resubmit. For revision, create a matrix with every editor and reviewer comment copied exactly, response, action, manuscript location, and evidence/status.

Respond in a calm, professional tone:

1. Thank the editor/reviewer briefly.
2. State whether you agree, partly agree, or disagree.
3. Explain the scientific reasoning and evidence.
4. State the exact change or justify no change.
5. Give stable page/line or section/table/figure locations.

Never claim a change that is absent. If comments conflict, prioritize the editor's instruction and explain the conflict. If a requested analysis is invalid, impossible, unethical, or outside scope, provide a bounded rebuttal and, where useful, add a limitation or alternative analysis. Re-audit the full manuscript because local changes can create global inconsistencies.

## Acceptance, proofs, and after publication

At proof stage, verify author names/order/ORCIDs, affiliations, equations, symbols, units, tables, figures, cross-references, hyperlinks, DOI metadata, funding, licences, and supplements. Limit proof changes to genuine corrections unless the editor authorizes more.

After publication, archive the accepted/published version as permitted, connect identifiers and repositories, share responsibly, and monitor substantive errors. Use the journal and COPE correction/retraction process when the record needs amendment; do not silently replace data or files that readers rely on.
